## Slide 1: VineWeave Canopy

VineWeave Canopy combines climbing plants with a tree-like support to provide pedestrian shade. Our group connects parametric geometry, material and construction choices, and shading analysis. The names below identify our responsibilities. We will explain both the design decisions and the evidence supporting them.

Q&A: Where are the full roles? The cover retains the core allocation; the planning document records the complete supplied descriptions.

## Slide 2: Define the final geometry—and how it is measured

The final geometry has an overall height of about three point four eight metres and a plan extent of six point two two by six point six six metres. Its approximate maximum horizontal exterior span is six point nine one metres. For these curved supports, inclination is defined by the endpoint chord above horizontal. The accompanying drawing sheets explain that definition and the measured range.

Transition: Next, link those features to environmental potential.

Q&A: Is 6.91 metres a circular diameter? No. It is the maximum pair distance in the projected exterior mesh, not a foliage diameter or net usable width.

## Slide 3: How the canopy can improve pedestrian comfort

The crown and foliage intercept direct sun before it reaches people and the ground. The solar arrows identify the pathway quantified in our optical scenarios. Blue arrows show potential air paths through open sides, not a simulated wind improvement. The dashed plant-water arrows indicate possible evapotranspiration, which depends on healthy plants and water. Denser foliage can also resist airflow.

Transition: Material assignment follows the structural roles.

Q&A: Do these arrows show simulated temperature or wind? No. They explain physical mechanisms; the project quantifies shade, not cooling degrees or wind speed.

## Slide 4: Assign materials to the roles they must perform

The take-off gives twelve ground-reaching supports and forty-seven crown members, with a total centre-line length of two hundred and eighty-eight point six six metres. We propose an eighty-by-four millimetre steel tube for supports and solid engineered bamboo for the crown. This is a material strategy, not proof that either zone is safe.

Transition: The screening table shows the inputs; the next decision is how to use them.

Q&A: Does the exterior model contain the final wall thickness? No. The pipe exterior and proposed material section must be distinguished.

## Slide 5: Read the material inputs before making the choice

These are preliminary screening inputs. Each cost column assumes the same full member length; it is not the adopted unit budget. Steel offers much greater nominal bending capacity. Engineered bamboo is proposed for the crown, but stiffness, treatment and product-specific evidence are essential. Raw bamboo’s low reference cost does not remove its variability and maintenance issues.

Transition: These inputs motivate a hybrid strategy, with conditions attached.

Q&A: Are these all verified design values? No. They are workbook screening inputs, and the bamboo identity and properties must be confirmed consistently.

## Slide 6: Why steel supports and an engineered-bamboo crown?

The workbook combines screening with a structural feasibility gate. Steel is assigned to supports under compression and wind bending. Bamboo is adopted for the crown using cost and carbon inputs, but the deflection sheet requires node rotational-stiffness verification. The lower cards summarise the five material criteria. Member-input savings do not establish a lower total unit cost.

Transition: The next check shows why crown stiffness makes the joints important.

Q&A: Does the material screen establish a lower total unit cost? No. It compares equal-length member inputs. The separate unit budget includes nodes, installation and care. Low conductivity also does not establish a measured temperature benefit.

## Slide 7: The beam check sets a requirement for the joint

The same six point six five metre bamboo-member calculation gives one hundred point five millimetres with simple supports and twenty point one with ideal fixed ends. The adopted target is thirty-three point two five millimetres, shown between them. This makes rotational restraint a design requirement to investigate. The proposed node does not prove that restraint, and the member check does not verify the whole frame.

Transition: The proposed node also has a significant budget allowance.

Q&A: What must be verified next? Product stiffness, node moment–rotation behaviour, load transfer and the whole frame under relevant loads. The supplied workbook explicitly requires a node detail and rotational-stiffness verification.

## Slide 8: The unit budget includes fabrication, installation and care

The preliminary total is approximately fifty-one thousand six hundred and seventy-six yuan. Materials are fifty-nine percent of this total; the other categories include fabrication, installation and care. Within materials, twelve proposed nodes account for twelve thousand yuan, more than the primary steel members. The component table is only part of the overall cost, not an additional total.

Transition: Next, the carbon and maintenance picture.

Q&A: Are the node prices confirmed? No. They are the most uncertain allowance and need a defined detail and supplier quotation.

## Slide 9: Account for the members, base and maintenance needs

The primary members and counterweight dominate the current carbon estimate. The counterweight contributes about thirty-nine percent, so foundation decisions also matter. We show the adopted estimate rather than claiming a verified life-cycle result. Outdoor performance also needs coating care, bamboo treatment and connection inspection. Product factors and the allowance for actual node materials still require checking.

Transition: Plant choice adds another set of requirements.

Q&A: Is stored biological carbon deducted? No. It is not credited in this estimate, and factor boundaries are not yet fully aligned.

## Slide 10: Keep the planting mix conditional on the site

The material schedule proposes two Boston ivy plants and one Rangoon creeper. Boston ivy is deciduous, so a summer canopy should not be presented as year-round cover. The source lists Rangoon creeper as a twining companion and trumpet creeper as an alternative. Local climate and attachment still need checking, as required by the analysis report. Guides, irrigation and pruning are part of the planting design.

Transition: Next, the optical method used to evaluate shade.

Q&A: Is this mix suitable for every city? No. The analysis report requires checking local climate, climbing method, root space and maintenance.

## Slide 11: Two shade metrics answer different questions

The first metric measures ideal leaf-envelope shadow on the target. The second includes frame shadows, assumed leaf patches, their overlap and transmittance. We use the first for the daily and solar-season result, and the second for parameter and coverage comparisons. The target is shared, but the percentages are not interchangeable. Neither is a temperature reduction.

Transition: First, read the ideal daily result across the full time window.

Q&A: What is the second formula? Aeq = Abare + (1 − τ)(Aleaf − Aoverlap), then divide by the target area. It is equivalent direct-beam blockage under the stated optical assumptions, not total solar-energy attenuation.

## Slide 12: A noon peak does not ensure all-day shade

The animation follows the existing solstice samples from nine to fifteen solar time. Overlap is about forty-seven percent at nine and fifteen, and almost ninety-nine percent at noon. The curve retains the full sequence and the equinox solar case, with time means of about seventy-five and fifty percent. The noon peak cannot stand for the whole use period: morning and afternoon exposure must be checked when placing seating. The equinox comparison changes solar conditions, not plant leaf cover.

Transition: We then change one geometric control at a time.

Q&A: Does the GIF add a new simulation? No. It animates the same ideal leaf-envelope projection at the existing 25 solar times. Its overlap values were checked against the unchanged solstice curve.

## Slide 13: Expanding the crown raises mean blockage: 52.3% → 66.1%

Seven cases change expansion, upper-height control or corner factor one at a time. At fifty percent cover, expansion changes mean blockage from fifty-two point three to sixty-six point one percent. The table retains the other results. The source report cautions that a larger crown at equal cover needs more planted area. These shade comparisons do not hold inputs equal or establish an optimum.

Transition: Next, keep the structure fixed and change foliage.

Q&A: Does the height control shift the whole model by 0.4 metres? No. It is smoothly weighted; actual maximum heights must be read from the geometry records.

## Slide 14: Coverage amount alone does not determine useful shade

The curve separates plan cover from mean direct-beam blockage. Bare members already provide shade at zero leaf cover. From eighty to ninety-five percent cover, mean blockage increases by only two point eight percentage points in these scenarios. Separately, at the same fifty percent cover, the three layouts give noon blockage from sixty-two point six to seventy-seven point five percent. The supplied report therefore asks us to consider leaf location and occupied-hour seating, not coverage alone.

Transition: We connect that moving footprint to public use.

Q&A: Why is 95% cover not 95% blockage? Target intersection, frame overlap, leaf placement and transmittance all affect the metric.

## Slide 15: Put occupied-hour shade into the layout decision

The animation holds cover fixed while the sun moves. Seating should be checked during occupied hours, with room for circulation, the counterweight, roots and maintenance. The next verification step is a real-site assessment with simultaneous shaded and unshaded environmental observations. The current analysis does not establish net usable shaded floor area or a measured comfort improvement.

Transition: The last design link is construction organisation.

Q&A: Are sensors a new project module here? No. This is the existing validation proposal, not a new monitoring-system design.

## Slide 16: Coordinate prefabricated modules and site interfaces

The proposal divides the system into crown members, supports, connections, base and planting. Numbering and preassembly help site organisation. However, the heavy counterweight governs lifting, and the sleeve-and-grout node requires curing and checks. The fourteen-person-day allowance is labour, not a calendar programme. A fully dry, losslessly reversible system should not be claimed.

Transition: Finally, our delivered work and its next checks.

Q&A: Is the node a finished construction detail? No. Its geometry, stiffness, fabrication and quality controls need to be resolved together.
